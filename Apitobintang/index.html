    Konsep kunci dalam bahasa sehari-hari

    Energi primer adalah sumber mentah (batu bara, sinar matahari, uranium). Energy carrier (pembawa energi) adalah bentuk yang kita kirim dan pakai, misalnya listrik, bensin, atau hidrogen. Efisiensi adalah berapa bagian energi yang benar-benar berguna; sisanya terbuang, biasanya sebagai panas. Energy density (kepadatan energi) adalah berapa banyak energi tersimpan dalam satu kilogram atau satu liter. Dispatchable berarti bisa dinyalakan atau diatur kapan saja; intermittent berarti naik-turun mengikuti cuaca. Baseload (beban dasar) adalah kebutuhan listrik minimum yang ada sepanjang waktu. Learning curve (kurva belajar) berarti makin banyak sesuatu dibuat, makin murah ia. Economies of scale (ekonomi skala) berarti produksi lebih besar menurunkan biaya per unit.

    Key concepts in everyday language

    Primary energy is the raw source (coal, sunlight, uranium). An energy carrier is the form we send and use, such as electricity, gasoline or hydrogen. Efficiency is the share of energy that actually becomes useful; the rest is lost, usually as heat. Energy density is how much energy is stored in one kilogram or one litre. Dispatchable means can be switched on or adjusted at any time; intermittent means rising and falling with the weather. Baseload is the minimum electricity demand that exists all the time. The learning curve means the more of something is made, the cheaper it gets. Economies of scale means larger production lowers cost per unit.

    Prinsip Energi: dari Sumber ke KegunaanPrinciples of Energy: from Source to Service

    Dengan kata sederhana
    Bayangkan energi seperti uang. Uang tidak hilang saat dibelanjakan, hanya berpindah tangan; energi juga begitu, hanya berubah bentuk: dari ikatan kimia di batu bara menjadi panas, lalu gerak, lalu listrik. Tiap perpindahan “dipotong biaya administrasi” berupa panas yang terbuang, dan potongan itu tidak bisa dihilangkan sepenuhnya. Bagian ini memperkenalkan rantai perpindahan itu, istilah dasarnya, dan alasan mengapa kepadatan energi menentukan bahan bakar mana yang cocok untuk pekerjaan apa.

    Energi tidak pernah “diproduksi” maupun “dihabiskan”. Hukum pertama termodinamika menyatakan jumlahnya kekal; yang berubah adalah bentuk dan kualitasnya. Hukum kedua menambahkan arah: setiap transformasi menyisakan sebagian energi sebagai panas pada suhu lingkungan, bentuk yang paling kurang berguna. Dalam bahasa teknik, yang dikonsumsi bukan energi, melainkan exergy (bagian energi yang masih bisa dipakai untuk kerja), yaitu bagian energi yang masih mampu melakukan kerja.

    Secara filosofis, peradaban adalah usaha mempertahankan keadaan yang jauh dari kesetimbangan (Schrödinger menyebutnya “negentropi”, Prigogine dissipative structure): tubuh, kota, dan pabrik bertahan hanya selama ada gradien yang dialirkan melaluinya. Mencari sumber energi berarti mencari gradien: gradien ikatan kimia pada fosil, gradien radiasi pada Matahari, gradien energi ikat inti pada atom. Seluruh buku ini adalah kisah pergeseran gradien itu.

    Rantai transformasi

    Setiap sistem energi dapat dibaca sebagai rantai: energi primer (batu bara, sinar matahari, uranium) → energy carrier (listrik, bensin, H₂, amonia) → energi final (yang terbaca di meteran atau tangki) → energi berguna (panas, gerak, cahaya) → layanan energi (mobilitas, kenyamanan termal, penerangan). Efisiensi total adalah hasil kali efisiensi tiap tautan; di sanalah sebagian besar kerugian bersembunyi. Mesin bensin mengubah sekitar 25–35% energi kimia menjadi gerak, motor listrik 85–95% dari listrik yang diterimanya (listriknya sendiri sudah melewati kerugian pembangkitan dan transmisi). Pompa panas memindahkan panas, bukan membangkitkannya, sehingga coefficient of performance (COP) dapat mencapai 3–4.

    Dari panas ke listrik: jalur termal-mekanik

    Sebagian besar listrik dunia, baik dari batu bara, gas, nuklir, biomassa, panas bumi, maupun panas matahari terkonsentrasi, tidak lahir langsung dari sumbernya. Sumber menghasilkan panas; panas memanaskan fluida kerja (air menjadi uap, atau udara dan gas buang) hingga bertekanan dan bersuhu tinggi; fluida berekspansi melewati sudu turbin (atau mendorong piston) dan menjadi gerak putar; poros memutar generator, tempat induksi elektromagnetik (Faraday, 1831) membuat elektron mengalir sebagai listrik. Logika ini bermula dari mesin uap Watt, disempurnakan turbin uap Parsons (1884), dan sejak pusat listrik awal 1880-an menjadi tulang punggung pembangkitan.

    Panas tidak bisa langsung menjadi kerja; batas Carnot menuntut fluida sepanas mungkin. Karena itu metalurgi dan tekanan menjadi batas teknologi: uap superkritis (di atas 221 bar, sekitar 600 °C) pada turbin uap, dan suhu masuk di atas 1.400 °C pada turbin gas. Ada dua siklus dasar: Rankine (siklus mesin uap: air jadi uap, memutar turbin, lalu diembunkan) (siklus tertutup, air diuapkan lalu dikondensasi) dan Brayton (siklus turbin gas: udara ditekan, dibakar, lalu memutar turbin) (siklus terbuka: kompresor, ruang bakar, turbin). Combined cycle (dua siklus ditumpuk: gas lalu uap) menyusun keduanya: gas buang Brayton memanaskan uap untuk Rankine, sehingga efisiensi mencapai 58–62%. Pada kendaraan, siklus Otto dan Diesel memakai piston, dan tekanan gas pembakaran menjadi gerak roda tanpa melewati listrik.

    In plain words
    Imagine energy as money. Money does not vanish when spent; it just changes hands. Energy is the same: it only changes form, from chemical bonds in coal to heat, then motion, then electricity. Each handover is “charged an admin fee” in the form of waste heat, and that fee cannot be removed entirely. This part introduces that chain of handovers, its basic terms, and why energy density decides which fuel suits which job.

    Energy is never “produced” or “used up”. The first law of thermodynamics says its amount is conserved; what changes is its form and quality. The second law adds a direction: every transformation leaves some energy as heat at ambient temperature, the least useful form. In engineering terms, what is consumed is not energy but exergy, the part of energy that can still do work.

    Philosophically, civilisation is an effort to maintain a state far from equilibrium (Schrödinger called it “negentropy”, Prigogine dissipative structures): bodies, cities and factories persist only while a gradient flows through them. To seek energy is to seek gradients: chemical bonds in fossil fuels, radiation from the Sun, nuclear binding energy in atoms. This whole book is the story of shifting those gradients.

    The chain of transformation

    Every energy system can be read as a chain: primary energy (coal, sunlight, uranium) → energy carrier (electricity, gasoline, H₂, ammonia) → final energy (what the meter or tank reads) → useful energy (heat, motion, light) → energy service (mobility, thermal comfort, lighting). Total efficiency is the product of the efficiency of each link; that is where most losses hide. A gasoline engine turns about 25–35% of chemical energy into motion, an electric motor 85–95% of the electricity it receives (though that electricity has already passed through generation and transmission losses). A heat pump moves heat rather than generating it, so its coefficient of performance (COP) can reach 3–4.

    From heat to electricity: the thermal-mechanical route

    Most of the world’s electricity, whether from coal, gas, nuclear, biomass, geothermal or concentrated solar heat, is not born directly from the source. The source produces heat; the heat warms a working fluid (water into steam, or air and exhaust gas) to high pressure and temperature; the fluid expands through turbine blades (or pushes a piston) and becomes rotation; the shaft turns a generator, where electromagnetic induction (Faraday, 1831) makes electrons flow as electricity. This logic began with Watt’s steam engine, was perfected by Parsons’ steam turbine (1884), and has been the backbone of generation since the first power stations of the 1880s.

    Heat cannot become work directly; the Carnot limit demands the hottest fluid possible. So metallurgy and pressure become technology limits: supercritical steam (above 221 bar, about 600 °C) in steam turbines, and inlet temperatures above 1,400 °C in gas turbines. There are two basic cycles: Rankine (closed cycle, water boiled and condensed) and Brayton (open cycle: compressor, combustor, turbine). Combined cycle stacks them: Brayton exhaust heats steam for the Rankine cycle, reaching 58–62% efficiency. In vehicles, Otto and Diesel cycles use pistons, and the pressure of combustion gas becomes wheel motion without passing through electricity.

    Batu bara, minyak, dan gas adalah sinar matahari purba yang tersimpan jutaan tahun. Saat dibakar, simpanan itu keluar sebagai panas. Panas inilah yang kita ubah menjadi gerak (mesin kendaraan) atau listrik (pembangkit). Bahan bakar fosil mendominasi karena sangat padat energi dan sudah punya jaringan pengiriman raksasa. Kelemahannya: pembakaran melepas CO₂, gas yang memerangkap panas di atmosfer, dan polutan yang merusak udara. Bab ini menjelaskan bagaimana panas menjadi gerak, mengapa sebagian energi pasti terbuang, dan apa saja yang keluar dari cerobong.

    1.1 Dari api ke gerak: bagaimana panas bekerja

    Tumbuhan dan plankton purba menyerap sinar matahari lewat fotosintesis dan menyimpannya dalam senyawa karbon dan hidrogen (disebut hidrokarbon). Selama jutaan tahun, tekanan dan panas bumi mengubahnya menjadi batu bara, minyak, dan gas. Jadi, setiap kali kita membakar bahan bakar fosil, kita sebenarnya memakai sinar matahari yang “ditabung” sejak zaman purba.

    Saat dibakar, bahan bakar bereaksi dengan oksigen. Karbonnya menjadi CO₂ (karbon dioksida) dan hidrogennya menjadi air. Ikatan kimia yang baru lebih stabil daripada yang lama, dan selisih energinya dilepas sebagai panas, mirip bola yang menggelinding turun bukit dan melepas energi geraknya.

    Panas saja belum berguna. Ia harus diubah menjadi gerak, lalu mungkin menjadi listrik. Di sini hukum alam memasang batas: panas tidak pernah bisa diubah seluruhnya menjadi gerak, karena selalu ada sisa yang dibuang ke lingkungan. Batas tertinggi untuk mesin panas disebut efisiensi Carnot:

    η = 1 − Tdingin ÷ Tpanas

    Suhunya memakai skala kelvin (K), yaitu derajat Celsius ditambah 273. Contoh: uap 600 °C (873 K) dengan lingkungan 27 °C (300 K) memberi batas sekitar 66%. Makin panas sumbernya, makin tinggi batasnya, tetapi mesin nyata selalu di bawah batas itu.

    Efisiensi berarti berapa persen energi bahan bakar yang menjadi hasil berguna; sisanya terbuang sebagai panas. Contoh nyata: pembangkit batu bara sekitar 33–40%, pembangkit gas siklus gabungan (CCGT, combined-cycle gas turbine) hingga sekitar 60%, dan mesin kendaraan sekitar 25–35%. Ingat angka ini. Di Bab III kita akan melihat bahwa surya dan angin menghindari tahap panas ini sama sekali.

    Sistem bahan bakar fosil punya tiga segmen. Hulu (upstream) adalah penemuan dan produksi: tambang dan sumur. Tengah (midstream) adalah pengangkutan: pipa, kapal tanker, dan terminal. Hilir (downstream) adalah kilang, distribusi, dan penjualan ke pengguna.

    Investasi selama seabad membuat biaya per unit rendah, tetapi juga menimbulkan lock-in (keadaan “terkunci”): mesin, pipa, dan kebiasaan sudah dibangun untuk fosil sehingga sulit berpindah.     • CO₂: gas rumah kaca (GRK) utama, penyebab pemanasan global.
    • NOx (oksida nitrogen): pemicu kabut asap dan hujan asam.
    • SOx (oksida belerang): berasal dari belerang dalam bahan bakar, pemicu hujan asam.
    • PM2.5: partikel sangat halus, sekitar sepertigapuluh lebar rambut, yang masuk ke paru-paru dan aliran darah, dan menjadi penyumbang utama penyakit pernapasan dan jantung.

    1.6 Konversi: dari api ke poros

    Setelah dibakar, panas bisa dipakai lewat tiga jalur: langsung sebagai panas (kompor, tungku), langsung sebagai gerak (mesin piston di kendaraan), atau lewat listrik (turbin dan generator). Jalur pertama tidak butuh konversi sama sekali, sehingga efisiensinya tinggi.

    Pembangkit batu bara menunjukkan jalur ketiga dengan jelas. Batu bara digiling halus dan dibakar di boiler (ketel). Panasnya mengubah air menjadi uap bertekanan sangat tinggi (superkritis: di atas 221 bar dan sekitar 600 °C; bar adalah satuan tekanan, 1 bar kira-kira tekanan udara di permukaan laut). Putarannya dijaga 3.000 putaran per menit (rpm), yaitu 50 putaran per detik, supaya cocok dengan frekuensi jaringan listrik 50 Hz (hertz = siklus per detik; sebagian negara memakai 60 Hz).

    In plain words
    Coal, oil and gas are ancient sunlight stored for millions of years. When burned, that stored energy comes out as heat, which we turn into motion (vehicle engines) or electricity (power plants). Fossil fuels dominate because they pack a lot of energy into little weight and already have a huge delivery network. The downside: burning releases CO₂, a gas that traps heat in the atmosphere, and pollutants that damage the air. This chapter explains how heat becomes motion, why some energy is always wasted, and what comes out of the chimney.

    1.1 From fire to motion: how heat does work

    Ancient plants and plankton absorbed sunlight through photosynthesis and stored it in compounds of carbon and hydrogen (called hydrocarbons). Over millions of years, pressure and heat turned them into coal, oil and gas. So whenever we burn a fossil fuel, we are using sunlight “saved” since ancient times.

    When fuel burns, it reacts with oxygen. The carbon becomes CO₂ (carbon dioxide) and the hydrogen becomes water. The new chemical bonds are more stable than the old ones, and the energy difference is released as heat, like a ball rolling downhill and giving up its energy.

    Heat alone is not useful. It must be turned into motion, and perhaps then into electricity. Here nature sets a limit: heat can never be turned entirely into motion, because some always has to be dumped into the surroundings. The highest possible efficiency of a heat engine is called the Carnot efficiency:

    η = 1 − Tcold ÷ Thot

    Temperatures use the kelvin (K) scale, which is degrees Celsius plus 273. Example: steam at 600 °C (873 K) with surroundings at 27 °C (300 K) gives a limit of about 66%. The hotter the source, the higher the limit, but real engines always fall below it.

    Efficiency means the share of a fuel’s energy that becomes useful output; the rest is lost as waste heat. Real examples: coal power plants about 33–40%, gas plants in combined-cycle mode (CCGT, combined-cycle gas turbine) up to about 60%, and vehicle engines about 25–35%. Keep these numbers in mind. In Chapter III we will see that solar and wind skip this heat step altogether.

    1.3 The supply chain: infrastructure already built

    The fossil fuel system has three segments. Upstream is discovery and production: mines and wells. Midstream is transport: pipelines, tankers and terminals. Downstream covers refineries, distribution and sales to users.

    A century of investment made unit costs low, but also created lock-in: machines, pipelines and habits were all built for fossil fuels, so switching is hard. One helpful measure is EROI (energy return on investment): the energy obtained compared with the energy spent to obtain it. In the twentieth century, easy oil gave a large return. As with gold mining, the easiest deposits run out first, so the return falls over time.

    1.4 Emissions and pollutants

    Burning releases several substances worth knowing:

    • CO₂: the main greenhouse gas (GHG), the driver of global warming.
    • NOx (nitrogen oxides): trigger smog and acid rain.
    • SOx (sulphur oxides): come from sulphur in the fuel and cause acid rain.
    • PM2.5: very fine particles, about one-thirtieth the width of a hair, that enter the lungs and bloodstream and are a major cause of respiratory and heart disease.

    So the reason to move away from fossil fuels is not only climate but also air quality. These two reasons will keep appearing as yardsticks in every later chapter.

    1.5 Energy density, infrastructure and logistics

    A 50-litre tank of gasoline holds about 1.7 GJ, or about 470 kWh of chemical energy (a kWh is the energy of a 1,000-watt device running for one hour). After an engine efficiency of about 30%, about 140 kWh remains as motion. This high density is why refuelling takes minutes and hundreds of kilometres feel normal.

    Delivery follows the fuel’s form. Coal moves as a solid by train, barge and bulk ship, and needs special handling (dust, risk of self-ignition). Oil and gas are easier to pump through pipelines and tankers. To avoid confusion, this book distinguishes three scales:

    1.6 Conversion: from fire to shaft

    Once fuel is burned, heat can be used in three ways: directly as heat (stoves, furnaces), directly as motion (piston engines in vehicles), or through electricity (turbine and generator). The first needs no conversion at all, so its efficiency is high.

    A coal power plant shows the third route clearly. Coal is ground fine and burned in a boiler. The heat turns water into steam at very high pressure (supercritical: above 221 bar and about 600 °C; bar is a unit of pressure, and 1 bar is roughly the air pressure at sea level). The steam pushes a turbine, a giant multi-stage fan, until it spins. The turbine shaft turns a generator: magnets spinning inside coils of wire create an electric current (Faraday’s induction, 1831). The spin is held at 3,000 revolutions per minute (rpm), or 50 per second, to match the 50 Hz frequency of the grid (hertz = cycles per second; some countries use 60 Hz).

    Efficiency here is a chain, so the numbers multiply: boiler ± 88–90%, steam cycle ± 40–45%, generator ± 98–99%. The end result is about 35–40%. In vehicles the path is shorter: combustion in the cylinder pushes the piston, the crankshaft turns the wheels, and no electricity is involved. Gas has its own route through the gas turbine, covered in Chapter II.

    1.7 Combustion: a few things worth understanding

    For fuel to burn completely, it needs the right amount of air. The exact ratio is called stoichiometric: about 14.7 parts air to 1 part gasoline (by mass), and about 17.2 for methane. The actual ratio is expressed as λ (lambda): λ = 1 is exact, above 1 means excess air, below 1 means too little air. Too little air produces CO (carbon monoxide) and soot; too much air makes the exhaust carry heat away.

    Pollutants follow flame conditions. CO and particles appear where air is short. Thermal NOx forms from nitrogen in the air and rises sharply above about 1,500 °C. SOx comes from sulphur in the fuel. So each technology has its own control:

    ControlUsed onFunction
    Three-way catalystGasoline enginesConverts CO, NOx and unburned fuel into safer substances; works at λ ≈ 1
    DPF and SCRDiesel enginesDPF (diesel particulate filter) traps particles; SCR (selective catalytic reduction) injects urea to cut NOx
    ESP and FGDCoal power plantsESP (electrostatic precipitator) captures dust with electricity; FGD (flue gas desulphurisation) removes sulphur
    Dry low-NOx burnersGas turbinesShape the flame to keep NOx low

    Two last ideas help with later chapters. First, burning produces water vapour, and that vapour carries hidden heat. The higher heating value (HHV) counts it; the lower heating value (LHV) does not. The difference is about 11% for methane (about 18% for hydrogen). A condensing boiler recovers that heat, so its efficiency can look above 100% when measured on an LHV basis. This does not break physics; it is only a matter of how we count.

    Second, combustion destroys exergy, the part of energy that can still do work. A flame at 1,500–2,000 °C is often used just to heat water to 60 °C. It is like using a chainsaw to slice bread: it works, but wastes capability. That is why heat pumps and fuel cells can beat combustion for certain tasks (see End Use).

Turning heat into motion or electricity is only about 25–60% efficient, and burning releases CO₂ and pollutants that harm the climate and air quality.     Mengganti seluruh energi fosil dalam semalam tidak mungkin. Listrik harus tersedia setiap detik, sedangkan surya dan angin naik-turun mengikuti cuaca. Karena itu dibutuhkan “jembatan”: pembangkit yang bisa dinyalakan kapan saja, terutama pembangkit gas yang lebih bersih daripada batu bara. Jembatan itu bisa dihijaukan dengan biometana (gas dari limbah organik) dan dengan CCS, yaitu menangkap CO₂ dari cerobong lalu menyimpannya di bawah tanah. Sebelum itu, bab ini memperkenalkan dulu mesin-mesin yang bekerja di dalam pembangkit: mesin uap, mesin bakar, dan turbin.

    2.1 Mesin panas: dari mesin uap sampai mesin bakar

    Hampir semua pembangkit dan kendaraan di bab ini memakai mesin panas, yaitu alat yang mengubah panas menjadi gerak. Prinsipnya selalu sama: gas atau uap yang panas dan bertekanan tinggi mengembang, dan saat mengembang ia mendorong sesuatu bergerak. Yang membedakan adalah letak apinya. Ada dua keluarga.

    Keluarga pertama: pembakaran di luar mesin (external combustion). Api menyala di luar, memanaskan air di dalam ketel (boiler) sehingga menjadi uap. Uap inilah yang bekerja. Contoh paling tua adalah mesin uap (steam engine). Uap bertekanan masuk ke sebuah silinder dan mendorong piston, yaitu sumbat logam yang bergerak maju-mundur. Gerak maju-mundur itu diubah menjadi putaran lewat batang penghubung dan roda gila. Setelah mendorong, uap dikeluarkan atau diembunkan kembali menjadi air. Mesin uap praktis pertama muncul di awal abad ke-18 untuk memompa air tambang. James Watt memperbaikinya pada akhir abad ke-18 dengan ruang pengembun terpisah yang menghemat banyak bahan bakar, dan sejak itu mesin uap menggerakkan pabrik, kereta, dan kapal.

    Pada 1884 Charles Parsons mengganti piston dengan turbin uap: uap disemprotkan melewati deretan sudu (bilah) yang terpasang pada sebuah poros, sehingga poros berputar halus dan sangat kuat. Hampir semua pembangkit uap modern memakai turbin ini, baik yang berbahan bakar batu bara, biomassa, panas bumi, maupun nuklir. Siklus kerjanya dinamai Rankine: air dipompa, dipanaskan menjadi uap, memutar turbin, diembunkan kembali menjadi air, lalu diulang. Karena air terus berputar di dalam sistem tertutup, bahan bakarnya boleh apa saja, yang penting menghasilkan panas.

    Keluarga kedua: pembakaran di dalam mesin (internal combustion), atau sering disebut mesin bakar. Api menyala di dalam mesin dan gas hasil pembakaran itu sendiri yang bekerja. Ini keluarga mesin bensin, mesin diesel, mesin gas, dan turbin gas. Cara kerja mesin piston yang paling umum, mesin empat langkah, adalah sebagai berikut:

    1. Isap: piston turun, udara dan bahan bakar masuk ke silinder.
    2. Kompresi: piston naik dan menekan campuran sehingga menjadi padat dan panas.
    3. Usaha: campuran menyala, gas panas mengembang dan mendorong piston turun. Hanya langkah inilah yang menghasilkan tenaga.
    4. Buang: piston naik lagi dan mengeluarkan gas bekas.

    Keempat langkah itu terjadi dalam dua putaran poros engkol, lalu berulang ratusan kali per menit. Pada mesin bensin (siklus Otto, 1876), campuran dinyalakan oleh percikan busi. Pada mesin diesel (siklus Diesel, 1890-an), hanya udara yang ditekan sampai sangat panas, lalu bahan bakar disemprot dan menyala sendiri. Karena diesel menekan lebih kuat (perbandingan tekanan sekitar 15–20 kali, dibanding sekitar 10 kali pada bensin), ia lebih efisien. Mesin gas mirip mesin bensin tetapi memakai gas seperti gas alam atau biogas, dan sering dipasang pada generator untuk menghasilkan listrik.

    Turbin gas adalah mesin bakar yang tidak memakai piston. Udara ditekan oleh kompresor, bahan bakar dibakar terus-menerus, dan gas panas memutar turbin. Prinsipnya sama dengan mesin jet pada pesawat, hanya saja sebagian besar tenaganya dipakai memutar generator. Siklus kerjanya dinamai Brayton.

    2.2 Mengapa transisi tidak bisa instan

    Jaringan listrik harus seimbang setiap detik: listrik yang dihasilkan harus sama dengan yang dipakai. Bila tidak seimbang, frekuensi jaringan bergeser dan, jika dibiarkan, listrik padam. Surya dan angin disebut variable renewable energy (VRE) karena keluarannya naik-turun mengikuti cuaca dan waktu (sifat ini disebut intermittent). Karena itu dibutuhkan pembangkit dispatchable, yaitu yang dapat dinyalakan dan diatur sesuai kebutuhan.

    Pada masa transisi, peran itu diisi terutama oleh pembangkit gas. Ada dua jenisnya. OCGT (open-cycle gas turbine, turbin gas siklus terbuka) adalah turbin gas sederhana yang bisa menyala dalam hitungan menit. Ia dipakai sebagai peaker plant, pembangkit yang dinyalakan saat kebutuhan listrik memuncak. CCGT (combined-cycle gas turbine, turbin gas siklus gabungan) lebih efisien dan dipakai lebih lama (penjelasannya di 2.7). Batu bara, sebaliknya, lambat dinaikkan atau diturunkan dan menghasilkan karbon paling banyak (lihat tabel di bagian Kerangka).

    Gas alam hampir seluruhnya terdiri dari metana (CH₄). Karena berupa gas, ia sangat renggang sehingga sulit dikirim jauh. Tiga cara mengatasinya:

Metana yang lolos ke udara menahan panas jauh lebih kuat daripada CO₂. Ukurannya disebut global warming potential (GWP, kekuatan memanaskan dibanding CO₂), dan pada rentang 20 tahun metana kira-kira 80 kali lebih kuat per kilogram.

    2.4 Biometana: gas dari limbah

    Limbah organik seperti sisa makanan, kotoran ternak, dan limbah cair pabrik akan membusuk bila ditutup tanpa udara. Bakteri mengubahnya menjadi biogas, campuran metana (sekitar 50–65%) dan CO₂. Proses ini disebut digesti anaerob (anaerobic digestion). Bila CO₂ dan pengotornya dibuang (upgrading), hasilnya biometana, yang secara kimia sama dengan gas alam.

    Karena sama, biometana bersifat drop-in, artinya bisa dipakai langsung tanpa mengubah peralatan: masuk pipa gas yang sama, dibakar di turbin dan kompor yang sama. Karbon yang dilepas berasal dari tumbuhan yang baru tumbuh, bukan dari perut bumi. Keterbatasannya adalah jumlah limbah yang tersedia, sehingga biometana melengkapi gas fosil, bukan menggantikannya.

    2.5 CCS: menangkap karbon sebelum lepas

    CCS (carbon capture and storage) bekerja dalam tiga langkah:

    1. Tangkap. Gas buang dilewatkan pelarut kimia (biasanya amina) yang menyerap CO₂; ini disebut post-combustion karena dilakukan setelah pembakaran. Pelarut lalu dipanaskan untuk melepaskan CO₂ murni. Tingkat penangkapan (capture rate) umumnya 85–95% dari gas buang.
    2. Angkut. CO₂ dimampatkan lalu dikirim lewat pipa atau kapal.
    3. Simpan. CO₂ disuntikkan jauh ke dalam tanah, sekitar satu kilometer atau lebih, ke lapisan batuan berpori berisi air asin (saline aquifer) atau ke ladang minyak dan gas yang sudah habis.

    Bila diterapkan pada steam methane reforming (SMR), yaitu pembuatan hidrogen dari gas alam dan uap yang melepas CO₂, hasilnya disebut hidrogen biru. Bila diterapkan pada pembuatan amonia, hasilnya amonia biru. Perlu diingat bahwa CCS mahal, memakai sebagian energi pembangkit (dijelaskan di 2.7), hanya menangkap CO₂ dari cerobong (bukan kebocoran metana di hulu), dan penyimpanannya harus dipantau dalam jangka panjang.

    2.6 Kepadatan energi, infrastruktur, dan logistik

    Gas sangat renggang, sehingga hampir seluruh biaya transisi gas terletak di logistik: pipa bertekanan tinggi (puluhan hingga sekitar 100 bar), fasilitas pencairan, kapal LNG (sekitar 170.000 m³ per kapal), dan terminal regasifikasi yang mengubah LNG kembali menjadi gas. Sebagian terminal berupa kapal terapung bernama FSRU (floating storage and regasification unit).

    CCS menambah lapisan logistik baru yang arahnya terbalik: karbon yang dulu ditambang kini harus diangkut kembali lewat pipa atau kapal menuju sumur injeksi. Karena itu pusat CCS (hub) pada praktiknya menjadi proyek infrastruktur bersama untuk satu kawasan industri.

    2.7 Konversi: turbin gas, siklus gabungan, dan sel bahan bakar

    Turbin gas bekerja dalam tiga bagian yang menyatu. Kompresor menekan udara sampai tekanannya naik sekitar 15–25 kali. Di ruang bakar, gas dibakar dalam udara bertekanan itu. Gas panas yang dihasilkan memutar turbin. Lebih dari separuh kerja turbin dipakai untuk memutar kompresor di poros yang sama, sisanya memutar generator. Mesin ini bisa menyala dalam hitungan menit karena massanya kecil, sedangkan boiler batu bara yang besar butuh berjam-jam untuk panas.

    Gas buang turbin gas masih sangat panas, sekitar 550–650 °C. Pada CCGT, panas itu tidak dibuang melainkan dialirkan ke heat recovery steam generator (HRSG, ketel uap yang memanfaatkan panas gas buang) untuk membuat uap yang memutar turbin uap. Dua mesin ditumpuk: siklus gas (Brayton) di depan dan siklus uap (Rankine) di belakang. Hasilnya efisiensi 58–62%, jauh di atas satu mesin sendirian.

    Biometana dapat dipakai di turbin yang sama. Hidrogen dan amonia hanya dapat dicampur sampai batas tertentu pada banyak turbin, karena nyalanya berbeda; pembakar harus disesuaikan untuk menghindari NOx tinggi dan flashback (nyala merambat balik ke dalam pembakar). Sel bahan bakar (fuel cell) menawarkan jalan pintas: hidrogen bereaksi dengan oksigen secara elektrokimia dan langsung menghasilkan listrik, tanpa piston dan tanpa turbin, dengan efisiensi listrik sekitar 40–60%. Cara mudah membayangkannya adalah baterai yang bahan kimianya terus diisi dari luar.

    CCS memberi penalti: pelarut amina harus dipanaskan untuk melepas CO₂, dan panasnya diambil dari uap pembangkit sendiri. Efisiensi turun sekitar 6–10 poin persentase (misalnya dari 58% menjadi sekitar 50%). Adapun hidrogen biru dibuat lewat SMR pada 700–1.000 °C, yaitu reaksi kimia, bukan siklus mesin panas.

    2.8 Pembangkit lokal berbasis pembakaran: sampah, biogas, biomassa

    Tidak semua pembangkit harus berkapasitas gigawatt. Di pulau terpencil, desa, kawasan industri, dan tempat pengolahan limbah, pembangkit kecil memakai sumber yang ada di sekitar. Secara kasar, pico berarti di bawah 5 kW, mikro hingga 100 kW, dan mini hingga 1–10 MW (batas istilahnya bervariasi). Prinsip konversinya sama dengan bagian 2.1: panas menjadi tekanan, tekanan menggerakkan turbin atau mesin, dan poros memutar generator. Yang berbeda adalah logistik. Sumber lokal mengurangi biaya BBM dan jaringan transmisi (Bab 5.3), tetapi biaya per kW umumnya lebih tinggi karena pembangkit kecil kehilangan keuntungan ukuran (economies of unit scale, Bab 5.7).

    In plain words
    Replacing all fossil energy overnight is impossible. Electricity must be available every second, while solar and wind rise and fall with the weather. So we need “bridges”: plants that can be switched on at any time, mainly gas plants, which are cleaner than coal. The bridge can be made greener with biomethane (gas from organic waste) and with CCS, which captures CO₂ from the chimney and stores it underground. First, though, this chapter introduces the machines that work inside power plants: steam engines, combustion engines and turbines.

    2.1 Heat engines: from the steam engine to the combustion engine

    Almost every power plant and vehicle in this chapter uses a heat engine: a device that turns heat into motion. The principle is always the same: hot, high-pressure gas or steam expands, and while expanding it pushes something. What differs is where the fire is. There are two families.

    Family one: combustion outside the engine (external combustion). The fire burns outside and heats water in a boiler to make steam. The steam does the work. The oldest example is the steam engine. Pressurised steam enters a cylinder and pushes a piston, a metal plug that moves back and forth. A connecting rod and flywheel turn that back-and-forth motion into rotation. After pushing, the steam is released or condensed back into water. The first practical steam engines appeared in the early 1700s to pump water out of mines. James Watt improved them in the late 1700s with a separate condenser that saved a lot of fuel, and steam engines went on to drive factories, trains and ships.

    In 1884 Charles Parsons replaced the piston with the steam turbine: steam is sprayed through rows of blades mounted on a shaft, so the shaft spins smoothly and powerfully. Almost all modern steam plants use this turbine, whether fuelled by coal, biomass, geothermal heat or nuclear. Its working cycle is called Rankine: water is pumped, heated into steam, spins the turbine, condenses back into water, and repeats. Because the water circulates in a closed system, any fuel will do, as long as it makes heat.

    Family two: combustion inside the engine (internal combustion), often called the combustion engine. The fire burns inside the machine and the combustion gas itself does the work. This is the family of gasoline engines, diesel engines, gas engines and gas turbines. The most common piston engine, the four-stroke engine, works like this:

    1. Intake: the piston moves down and air and fuel enter the cylinder.
    2. Compression: the piston rises and squeezes the mixture, making it dense and hot.
    3. Power: the mixture ignites, and hot gas expands and pushes the piston down. Only this stroke produces power.
    4. Exhaust: the piston rises again and pushes out the spent gas.

    The four strokes take two turns of the crankshaft and repeat hundreds of times per minute. In a gasoline engine (Otto cycle, 1876), a spark plug ignites the mixture. In a diesel engine (Diesel cycle, 1890s), only air is compressed until it is very hot, then fuel is sprayed in and ignites by itself. Because diesel compresses harder (about 15–20 times, against about 10 times for gasoline), it is more efficient. A gas engine is similar to a gasoline engine but burns gas such as natural gas or biogas, and is often fitted to generators to produce electricity.

    A gas turbine is a combustion engine without pistons. A compressor squeezes air, fuel burns continuously, and the hot gas spins a turbine. The principle is the same as a jet engine, except that most of the power drives a generator. Its cycle is called Brayton.

    2.2 Why the transition cannot be instant

    The grid must balance every second: electricity produced must equal electricity used. If not, grid frequency drifts and, if left alone, power fails. Solar and wind are called variable renewable energy (VRE) because their output rises and falls with weather and time of day (this trait is called intermittent). So we need dispatchable plants, which can be started and adjusted on demand.

    During the transition, gas plants fill this role. There are two types. OCGT (open-cycle gas turbine) is a simple gas turbine that can start within minutes. It is used as a peaker plant, switched on when demand peaks. CCGT (combined-cycle gas turbine) is more efficient and runs for longer (explained in 2.7). Coal, by contrast, is slow to ramp up or down and emits the most carbon (see the table in the Framework section).

    2.3 Gas: LNG, CNG and LPG

    Natural gas is almost entirely methane (CH₄). Being a gas, it is very thin and hard to ship far. There are three ways around this:

One critical note: gas beats coal only if methane leakage (methane escaping from wells, pipes and joints) is low. Methane that escapes traps heat far more strongly than CO₂. The measure is called global warming potential (GWP, heat-trapping strength compared with CO₂), and over 20 years methane is roughly 80 times stronger per kilogram.

    2.4 Biomethane: gas from waste

    Organic waste such as food scraps, animal manure and factory wastewater rots when sealed away from air. Bacteria turn it into biogas, a mix of methane (about 50–65%) and CO₂. This process is called anaerobic digestion. If the CO₂ and impurities are removed (upgrading), the result is biomethane, chemically the same as natural gas.

    Because it is the same, biomethane is a drop-in: it can be used directly without changing equipment, entering the same gas pipes and burning in the same turbines and stoves. The carbon released comes from recently grown plants, not from underground. Its limit is the amount of waste available, so biomethane complements fossil gas rather than replacing it.

    2.5 CCS: capturing carbon before it escapes

    CCS (carbon capture and storage) works in three steps:

    1. Capture. Exhaust gas passes through a chemical solvent (usually an amine) that absorbs CO₂; this is post-combustion capture because it happens after burning. The solvent is then heated to release pure CO₂. The capture rate is typically 85–95% of the exhaust gas.
    2. Transport. The CO₂ is compressed and sent by pipeline or ship.
    3. Storage. The CO₂ is injected deep underground, about a kilometre or more, into porous rock layers filled with salty water (saline aquifers) or into depleted oil and gas fields.

    Applied to steam methane reforming (SMR), which makes hydrogen from natural gas and steam and releases CO₂, the result is called blue hydrogen. Applied to ammonia production, it gives blue ammonia. Keep in mind that CCS is costly, uses part of the plant’s energy (explained in 2.7), captures only CO₂ from the chimney (not upstream methane leaks), and requires long-term monitoring of the storage.

    2.6 Energy density, infrastructure and logistics

    Gas is very thin, so nearly all the cost of the gas transition lies in logistics: high-pressure pipelines (tens up to about 100 bar), liquefaction plants, LNG ships (about 170,000 m³ each), and regasification terminals that turn LNG back into gas. Some terminals are floating ships called FSRUs (floating storage and regasification units).

    CCS adds a new layer of logistics that runs in reverse: carbon that was once mined must now be carried back by pipeline or ship to injection wells. So a CCS hub becomes, in practice, a shared infrastructure project for an industrial area.

    2.7 Conversion: gas turbines, combined cycle and fuel cells

    A gas turbine has three parts working as one. The compressor squeezes air until its pressure is about 15–25 times higher. In the combustor, gas burns in that pressurised air. The resulting hot gas spins the turbine. More than half of the turbine’s work drives the compressor on the same shaft, and the rest turns the generator. It can start within minutes because it is light, whereas a large coal boiler takes hours to heat up.

    The exhaust of a gas turbine is still very hot, about 550–650 °C. In a CCGT, that heat is not wasted but sent to a heat recovery steam generator (HRSG, a boiler that uses exhaust heat) to make steam for a steam turbine. Two engines are stacked: a gas cycle (Brayton) in front and a steam cycle (Rankine) behind. The result is 58–62% efficiency, far above either engine alone.

    Biomethane can run in the same turbines. Hydrogen and ammonia can only be blended up to a limit in many turbines, because they burn differently; burners must be adapted to avoid high NOx and flashback (flame travelling back into the burner). A fuel cell offers a shortcut: hydrogen reacts with oxygen electrochemically and directly produces electricity, with no piston and no turbine, at about 40–60% electrical efficiency. An easy picture is a battery whose chemicals are refilled from outside.

    CCS carries a penalty: the amine solvent must be heated to release CO₂, and that heat is taken from the plant’s own steam. Efficiency drops by about 6–10 percentage points (for example from 58% to about 50%). Blue hydrogen, meanwhile, is made through SMR at 700–1,000 °C, a chemical reaction rather than a heat-engine cycle.

    2.8 Local combustion plants: waste, biogas and biomass

    Not every power plant needs gigawatts of capacity. On remote islands, in villages, industrial estates and waste-treatment sites, small plants use what is around them. Roughly, pico means below 5 kW, micro up to 100 kW, and mini up to 1–10 MW (the boundaries vary). The conversion principle is the same as in 2.1: heat becomes pressure, pressure drives a turbine or engine, and the shaft turns a generator. What differs is logistics. Local sources cut fuel and transmission costs (Chapter 5.3), but cost per kW is usually higher because small plants lose the advantage of size (economies of unit scale, Chapter 5.7).

Gas, biomethane and CCS buy time because they can use existing infrastructure, despite energy penalties, costs and leakage risks. Blue hydrogen is a bridge to green hydrogen (Chapter III), and CCS’s ability to cut costs will be decided by the laws of scale in Chapter V.

    3.1 Sumber primer terbarukan

    Empat sumber utama bekerja dengan cara berbeda. Panel surya memakai efek fotovoltaik (photovoltaic, PV): cahaya yang mengenai sel silikon melepaskan elektron, dan elektron itu mengalir sebagai arus listrik searah. Tidak ada bagian yang bergerak. Modul komersial mengubah sekitar 20–24% cahaya menjadi listrik, dan batas teoretis sel silikon tunggal sekitar 33% (disebut batas Shockley–Queisser). Turbin angin menangkap gerak udara dengan bilah rotor yang memutar generator. Fisika membatasi rotor paling banyak mengambil 59,3% energi angin (batas Betz); turbin nyata mengambil sekitar 35–50%. Air dan panas bumi dibahas di 3.6 dan 3.11.

Ukurannya adalah faktor kapasitas (capacity factor): persentase listrik nyata dibanding bila pembangkit selalu berdaya penuh sepanjang tahun. Panel surya dengan faktor kapasitas 20% setara dengan bekerja penuh sekitar 4,8 jam per hari.     3.2 Jalur elektron: baterai

    Baterai litium-ion menyimpan energi dalam ikatan kimia. Saat diisi, ion litium bergerak dari satu kutub (katoda) ke kutub lain (anoda) melalui cairan penghantar; saat dipakai, ion kembali dan melepaskan elektron sebagai arus listrik. Tidak ada bagian yang bergerak. Dua jenis yang paling umum adalah LFP (litium besi fosfat; aman, murah, tanpa kobalt dan nikel) dan NMC (nikel mangan kobalt; lebih padat energi).

    Keunggulan baterai adalah efisiensi siklus (round-trip efficiency, yaitu berapa persen listrik yang bisa diambil kembali setelah disimpan): sekitar 85–95% pada tingkat sel dan 80–90% pada tingkat sistem. Kelemahannya adalah kepadatan energi. Energi per kilogram sel sekitar 160–200 Wh (LFP) dan 200–280 Wh (NMC), atau sekitar 0,6–1,0 MJ/kg, jauh di bawah bensin (± 46 MJ/kg, Bab I). Karena motor listrik jauh lebih efisien daripada mesin bensin, selisih praktisnya lebih kecil dari angka itu, tetapi tetap besar.

    Akibatnya, baterai unggul untuk kendaraan ringan dan untuk BESS (battery energy storage system, sistem penyimpanan energi baterai) di jaringan yang menyimpan listrik beberapa jam. Bobot membatasi pemakaiannya pada pesawat jarak jauh, kapal samudra, dan truk tambang.

    3.3 Jalur molekul: hidrogen hijau

    Jalur kedua menyimpan listrik dalam bentuk molekul. Elektrolisis memakai listrik untuk memecah air (H₂O) menjadi hidrogen (H₂) dan oksigen (O₂). Bila listriknya terbarukan, hasilnya disebut hidrogen hijau. Secara teori 1 kg hidrogen butuh sekitar 33 kWh, tetapi sistem nyata memakai sekitar 50–55 kWh. Bila hidrogen itu kemudian diubah kembali menjadi listrik, hanya sekitar 30–40% energi awal yang kembali.

    Efisiensi serendah itu hanya masuk akal untuk sesuatu yang tidak bisa diberikan baterai: hidrogen sangat ringan per energi (sekitar 120 MJ/kg), bisa disimpan lama dalam jumlah besar, serta dapat menyediakan panas proses dan bahan kimia untuk industri berat seperti baja dan pupuk. Rincian produksi, penyimpanan, dan keekonomiannya ada di 3.8 sampai 3.10.

    3.4 Power-to-X

    Power-to-X berarti mengubah listrik menjadi produk lain (“X”) yang lebih mudah disimpan atau dipakai. Hidrogen bisa direaksikan dengan CO₂ menjadi e-metana (reaksi Sabatier), atau dengan nitrogen menjadi amonia (proses Haber–Bosch), atau diolah menjadi e-fuel, bahan bakar cair sintetis. Setiap langkah konversi menambah kerugian energi. Imbalannya, produk akhirnya mudah disimpan berbulan-bulan, sehingga cocok untuk penyimpanan musiman (seasonal storage): kelebihan listrik surya di musim terang disimpan seperti padi di lumbung untuk dipakai di musim gelap.

    3.5 Densitas, infrastruktur, dan logistik

    Terbarukan membalik masalah logistik fosil. Daya yang dihasilkan per luas lahan kecil: sekitar 5–10 W/m² untuk panel surya dan 1–3 W/m² untuk angin, jauh di bawah sumur gas atau tambang batu bara. Karena itu energi harus “dipanen” di area luas lalu dikirim lewat kabel. Infrastruktur utamanya bukan lagi pipa dan kapal, melainkan jaringan listrik: kabel transmisi (HVDC, arus searah bertegangan tinggi, dipakai untuk jarak jauh), gardu, dan penyimpanan.     3.6 Konversi: tiga arketipe

    Terbarukan menghasilkan listrik lewat tiga pola yang berbeda dari fosil.

    (a) Gerak tanpa panas. Angin memutar rotor yang menggerakkan generator, langsung atau lewat gearbox (roda gigi penambah putaran). Pada hidro, air yang jatuh dari ketinggian menjadi tekanan dan aliran yang memutar turbin (jenis Pelton, Francis, atau Kaplan menurut ketinggian jatuh) dengan efisiensi 85–92%. Prinsipnya sama dengan mesin uap, hanya fluidanya air atau udara dan tidak ada pembakaran.

    (b) Langsung ke elektron. Sel PV menghasilkan arus searah (DC) tanpa bagian bergerak. Inverter, yaitu alat pengubah arus searah menjadi arus bolak-balik (AC), lalu menyesuaikannya dengan jaringan.

    (c) Kembali ke uap. Panas bumi memakai uap alami dari sumur (suhu 150–300 °C) untuk memutar turbin, atau memakai siklus Rankine organik (ORC) yang memakai fluida khusus untuk suhu lebih rendah. CSP (concentrated solar power) memusatkan cahaya dengan cermin menjadi panas, menyimpannya dalam garam leleh, lalu memutar turbin uap saat dibutuhkan.

    Penyimpanan mengikuti pola serupa. Baterai bekerja secara elektrokimia tanpa turbin. Pumped hydro memakai listrik berlebih untuk memompa air ke atas, lalu menjatuhkannya lewat turbin saat dibutuhkan. Elektroliser mengubah elektron menjadi ikatan kimia, dan sel bahan bakar membalikkannya.

    Perbedaan ini berdampak pada jaringan. Generator putar membawa inersia, yaitu kelembaman massa berputar yang meredam perubahan frekuensi mendadak. PV dan baterai terhubung lewat elektronika daya sehingga tidak punya inersia alami, dan jaringan butuh grid-forming inverter (inverter yang ikut menjaga tegangan dan frekuensi). Hal ini dibahas lagi di Bab 5.7.

    3.8 Hidrogen: sumber, warna, dan elektroliser

Jadi “ekonomi hidrogen” sebenarnya sudah ada. Yang baru adalah membuatnya tanpa karbon dan memakainya di tempat baru.

Air di dasar pipa pesat (penstock, pipa yang mengalirkan air ke turbin) bertekanan sekitar 2 bar.

    Jenis turbin mengikuti tinggi jatuh: Pelton untuk tinggi jatuh besar, crossflow (Banki) dan Francis untuk menengah, serta Kaplan, kincir air, dan sekrup Archimedes untuk tinggi jatuh rendah. Ada pula turbin yang ditaruh langsung di arus sungai tanpa tinggi jatuh. Bendungan memberi satu hal yang tidak dimiliki aliran sungai: penyimpanan. Sebaliknya, pembangkit aliran sungai tanpa waduk besar (run-of-river) dampaknya jauh lebih kecil tetapi produksinya mengikuti musim. Pumped hydro skala kecil memakai prinsip yang sama untuk menyimpan listrik surya siang hari.

    3.12 Angin dan laut

    Daya angin dihitung dengan rumus berikut, dibaca “setengah × massa jenis udara × luas sapuan rotor × kecepatan angin pangkat tiga × efisiensi rotor (Cp)”:

    P = ½ · ρ · A · v³ · Cp

    Kecepatan angin dipangkatkan tiga, sehingga dua kali lebih cepat berarti delapan kali lebih besar daya. Rotor berdiameter 5 m pada 5 m/s menghasilkan sekitar 0,5 kW; pada 10 m/s sekitar 4 kW. Itu sebabnya memilih lokasi lebih penting daripada memilih merek turbin. Itu pula sebabnya kincir pompa air mekanis masih berguna: ia menggerakkan pompa langsung tanpa generator dan listrik.

    Gelombang. Pada oscillating water column (OWC), gelombang naik-turun di dalam sebuah ruang sehingga menekan dan menarik udara melewati turbin Wells, turbin khusus yang tetap berputar satu arah walau udara bolak-balik. Pada overtopping, gelombang mengisi bak yang tinggi lalu airnya dijatuhkan, mirip pembangkit air bertinggi jatuh rendah. Pasang surut adalah yang paling mudah diprediksi, tetapi hanya di selat dan teluk tertentu. Bendung pasang surut seperti La Rance di Prancis (240 MW) bekerja seperti bendungan, sedangkan turbin arus (misalnya MeyGen di Skotlandia) bekerja seperti kincir angin di bawah air. OTEC (ocean thermal energy conversion) memanfaatkan selisih suhu air permukaan dan air dalam sekitar 20 K di perairan tropis. Batas Carnot-nya rendah, tetapi sumbernya stabil sepanjang tahun.

    3.13 Integrasi mikrogrid dan pemilihan teknologi

    Generator kecil punya inersia kecil, sehingga frekuensinya mudah bergoyang. Pembangkit mikrohidro memakai electronic load controller, pengatur beban yang menjaga frekuensi dengan membuang kelebihan daya ke beban penahan. Mikrogrid pulau biasanya menggabungkan panel surya, baterai, dan sumber yang bisa diatur (hidro, biogas, biomassa, atau diesel sebagai cadangan).

    Kriteria pemilihan yang realistis ada empat: sumber lokal yang tersedia sepanjang tahun, pola pemakaian listrik, kemampuan merawat pembangkit di tempat (suku cadang dan tenaga terampil), dan dampak lingkungan setempat. Pembangkit kecil yang mangkrak karena tidak ada perawatan jauh lebih sering terjadi daripada kegagalan teknologinya.

    In plain words
    Green sources harvest nature’s flows: sunlight, wind, running water and heat from inside the Earth. Solar panels turn light directly into electricity with no moving parts, while wind and water spin turbines without burning anything. The challenge is storage, because the sun does not shine at night and wind does not always blow. There are two storage routes: batteries (store electricity; efficient but heavy) and hydrogen (stores energy in chemical bonds; less efficient but long-lasting and suited to heavy duties). This chapter covers both, treats hydrogen in depth, and ends with small hydro, wind and ocean plants.

    3.1 Primary renewable sources

    Four main sources work in different ways. Solar panels use the photovoltaic effect (PV): light hitting a silicon cell frees electrons, and those electrons flow as direct current. There are no moving parts. Commercial modules convert about 20–24% of light to electricity, and the theoretical limit of a single silicon cell is about 33% (the Shockley–Queisser limit). Wind turbines catch moving air with rotor blades that turn a generator. Physics limits a rotor to at most 59.3% of the wind’s energy (the Betz limit); real turbines capture about 35–50%. Water and geothermal heat are covered in 3.6 and 3.11.

    The most important difference is not efficiency but how often the source works. The measure is the capacity factor: the share of electricity actually produced compared with running at full power all year. A solar panel with a 20% capacity factor is equivalent to working at full power about 4.8 hours a day. A source with a high, steady capacity factor is said to provide firm power (power you can rely on).

    3.2 The electron route: batteries

    A lithium-ion battery stores energy in chemical bonds. When charging, lithium ions move from one electrode (the cathode) to the other (the anode) through a conducting liquid; when discharging, the ions return and release electrons as electric current. There are no moving parts. The two most common types are LFP (lithium iron phosphate; safe, cheap, no cobalt or nickel) and NMC (nickel manganese cobalt; more energy-dense).

    A battery’s strength is its round-trip efficiency (the share of electricity you can get back after storing it): about 85–95% at cell level and 80–90% at system level. Its weakness is energy density. Cells hold about 160–200 Wh per kilogram (LFP) and 200–280 Wh (NMC), or about 0.6–1.0 MJ/kg, far below gasoline (± 46 MJ/kg, Chapter I). Because electric motors are much more efficient than gasoline engines, the practical gap is smaller than those numbers suggest, but still large.

    As a result, batteries excel in light vehicles and in BESS (battery energy storage systems) on the grid, storing electricity for a few hours. Weight limits their use in long-haul aircraft, ocean-going ships and mining trucks.

    3.3 The molecule route: green hydrogen

    The second route stores electricity as a molecule. Electrolysis uses electricity to split water (H₂O) into hydrogen (H₂) and oxygen (O₂). If the electricity is renewable, the result is called green hydrogen. In theory 1 kg of hydrogen needs about 33 kWh, but real systems use about 50–55 kWh. If the hydrogen is later turned back into electricity, only about 30–40% of the original energy returns.

    Efficiency this low makes sense only for something batteries cannot give: hydrogen is very light per unit of energy (about 120 MJ/kg), can be stored for a long time in large amounts, and can supply process heat and chemicals for heavy industry such as steel and fertiliser. Details of production, storage and economics are in 3.8 to 3.10.

    3.4 Power-to-X

    Power-to-X means converting electricity into another product (“X”) that is easier to store or use. Hydrogen can be reacted with CO₂ to make e-methane (the Sabatier reaction), with nitrogen to make ammonia (the Haber–Bosch process), or processed into e-fuels, synthetic liquid fuels. Each conversion step adds energy losses. In return, the end product is easy to store for months, which suits seasonal storage: surplus solar electricity from bright seasons is stored like rice in a granary for use in dark seasons.

    3.5 Density, infrastructure and logistics

    Renewables flip the logistics problem of fossil fuels. Power produced per land area is small: about 5–10 W/m² for solar panels and 1–3 W/m² for wind, far below a gas well or coal mine. So energy must be “harvested” over large areas and sent by cable. The main infrastructure is no longer pipes and ships but the electricity grid: transmission cables (HVDC, high-voltage direct current, used over long distances), substations and storage. Batteries serve hours; molecules serve seasons.

    3.6 Conversion: three archetypes

    Renewables make electricity in three patterns that differ from fossil fuels.

    (a) Motion without heat. Wind turns a rotor that drives a generator, directly or through a gearbox (gears that raise the rotation speed). In hydro, water falling from a height becomes pressure and flow that spins a turbine (Pelton, Francis or Kaplan, depending on the height) at 85–92% efficiency. The principle is the same as the steam engine, but the fluid is water or air and nothing is burned.

    (b) Straight to electrons. PV cells produce direct current (DC) with no moving parts. An inverter, a device that converts direct current to alternating current (AC), then matches it to the grid.

    (c) Back to steam. Geothermal uses natural steam from wells (150–300 °C) to spin a turbine, or an organic Rankine cycle (ORC) with a special fluid for lower temperatures. CSP (concentrated solar power) focuses sunlight with mirrors into heat, stores it in molten salt, and spins a steam turbine when needed.

    Storage follows similar patterns. Batteries work electrochemically without turbines. Pumped hydro uses surplus electricity to pump water uphill, then drops it through a turbine when needed. Electrolysers turn electrons into chemical bonds, and fuel cells reverse that.

    These differences matter for the grid. Spinning generators carry inertia, the sluggishness of a rotating mass that damps sudden frequency changes. PV and batteries connect through power electronics and have no natural inertia, so grids need grid-forming inverters (inverters that help hold voltage and frequency). This returns in Chapter 5.7.

    Hydrogen’s energy per volume is only about a third of methane’s. So if hydrogen is blended at 20% by volume into a gas grid, it contributes only about 7% of the energy. For use, the choice depends on the job: fuel cells (electrical efficiency ± 50–60%), hydrogen piston engines (± 40–45%), and gas turbines being developed toward 100% hydrogen.

    One critical note: hydrogen is only clean if its source is clean. Grey hydrogen (made from natural gas without CCS) releases about 9–12 kg of CO₂ per kg of H₂, equal to about 75–100 kg of CO₂ per GJ, higher than burning natural gas directly (about 56). Burning hydrogen moves emissions from the chimney upstream.

    3.8 Hydrogen: sources, colours and electrolysers

    Hydrogen is not an energy source but an energy carrier: on Earth it is almost entirely bound in water and hydrocarbons, so energy must first be spent to release it. The world already uses about 90–100 million tonnes of hydrogen a year, mainly for oil refining, fertiliser (ammonia) and methanol, and nearly all of it is made from gas and coal without carbon capture. So the “hydrogen economy” already exists. What is new is making it without carbon and using it in new places.

    P = ρ · g · Q · H · η

    Here ρ is the density of water (1,000 kg/m³), g is gravity (9.81), Q is the water flow (m³ per second), H is the height of fall (m, called the head), and η is efficiency. Water at the bottom of the penstock (the pipe carrying water to the turbine) is at about 2 bar.

    The turbine type follows the head: Pelton for high head, crossflow (Banki) and Francis for medium, and Kaplan, water wheels and Archimedes screws for low head. Some turbines sit directly in river currents with no head at all. A dam gives something a river does not: storage. In contrast, run-of-river plants (no large reservoir) have far less impact but their output follows the seasons. Small-scale pumped hydro uses the same principle to store daytime solar electricity.

    3.12 Wind and sea

    Wind power is calculated with this formula, read “half × air density × rotor swept area × wind speed cubed × rotor efficiency (Cp)”:

    P = ½ · ρ · A · v³ · Cp

    Wind speed is cubed, so doubling it means eight times the power. A rotor 5 m across produces about 0.5 kW at 5 m/s and about 4 kW at 10 m/s. That is why choosing the site matters more than choosing the turbine brand. It is also why mechanical water-pumping windmills remain useful: they drive the pump directly, without a generator or electricity.

    Waves. In an oscillating water column (OWC), waves rise and fall inside a chamber, pushing and pulling air through a Wells turbine, a special turbine that keeps spinning one way even though the air flows back and forth. In overtopping, waves fill a raised basin and the water is then dropped, like a low-head hydro plant. Tides are the most predictable, but only in certain straits and bays. Tidal barrages such as La Rance in France (240 MW) work like dams, while current turbines (for example MeyGen in Scotland) work like windmills under water. OTEC (ocean thermal energy conversion) uses the temperature difference of about 20 K between surface and deep water in tropical seas. Its Carnot limit is low, but its source is steady all year.

    3.13 Microgrid integration and choosing technology

    Small generators have little inertia, so their frequency swings easily. Micro-hydro plants use an electronic load controller, which holds frequency by dumping surplus power into a ballast load. Island microgrids usually combine solar panels, batteries and dispatchable sources (hydro, biogas, biomass, or diesel as backup).

    Four realistic selection criteria: a local source available all year, the pattern of electricity use, the ability to maintain the plant on site (spare parts and skilled people), and local environmental impact. Small plants abandoned for lack of maintenance are far more common than failures of the technology itself.

    Green sources work in three patterns: motion without heat (wind, water), straight to electrons (solar, batteries, fuel cells), and back to steam (geothermal, CSP). Batteries excel for the light and daily; hydrogen for the heavy, distant and hot, but must be chosen with precision because its energy losses are large. The shared weakness of both, density and cost, drives the search in Chapter IV, and the ability to produce them at scale is tested in Chapter V.

    4.1 Dua cara melepas energi inti: fisi dan fusi

    Inti atom diikat oleh gaya yang sangat kuat. Dalam reaksi inti, massa hasil reaksi sedikit lebih kecil daripada massa awalnya. Selisih massa itu tidak lenyap, melainkan berubah menjadi energi menurut rumus terkenal Einstein:

    E = Δm · c²

    Dibaca: energi sama dengan selisih massa dikali kecepatan cahaya kuadrat. Karena kecepatan cahaya sangat besar, selisih massa yang amat kecil sudah menghasilkan energi yang sangat besar.

    Fisi (fission) membelah inti berat, misalnya uranium-235, menjadi dua inti yang lebih kecil. Saat membelah, inti itu juga melepas neutron (partikel tanpa muatan), dan neutron itu dapat membelah inti lain, sehingga terjadi reaksi berantai. Di dalam reaktor, reaksi berantai ini dijaga tetap terkendali oleh batang kendali yang menyerap neutron. Satu jalur pengembangan adalah SMR (small modular reactor), reaktor kecil yang dibuat di pabrik lalu dirakit di lokasi, dengan harapan menurunkan biaya melalui produksi berulang.

Bahan yang paling menjanjikan adalah deuterium (hidrogen berat, tersedia di air laut) dan tritium (hidrogen yang lebih berat lagi, langka, dan harus dibiakkan dari litium). Reaksi D–T menghasilkan helium, satu neutron, dan 17,6 MeV energi (MeV adalah juta elektronvolt, satuan energi pada skala atom).     4.2 Fusi: menyalakan bintang dalam botol

    Dua inti bermuatan positif saling menolak. Untuk menyatukannya, keduanya harus dipacu dengan kecepatan yang sangat tinggi, dan itu berarti suhu yang luar biasa. Di Matahari, tekanan raksasa membantu sehingga cukup sekitar 15 juta °C. Di Bumi tekanan seperti itu tidak bisa dibuat, jadi suhu harus jauh lebih tinggi: sekitar 100 juta °C. Pada suhu itu bahan berubah menjadi plasma, gas yang sangat panas di mana elektron terlepas dari atomnya sehingga bermuatan listrik. Tidak ada dinding yang tahan menyentuhnya, jadi plasma harus dikurung oleh medan magnet agar melayang di tengah ruang.

    Ada tiga syarat yang harus dipenuhi bersamaan: plasma harus cukup panas, cukup rapat, dan terkurung cukup lama. Hasil kali ketiganya disebut triple product, dan batas minimum agar fusi menghasilkan energi disebut Lawson criterion. Dua jenis alat pengurung bersaing:

    • Tokamak: ruang berbentuk donat. Arus listrik di dalam plasma ikut membentuk medan magnet pengurung.
    • Stellarator: ruang donat yang medannya dibentuk seluruhnya oleh kumparan magnet berbentuk rumit dan terpilin. Lebih stabil, tetapi lebih sulit dibuat; contohnya Wendelstein 7-X di Jerman.

    Magnet superkonduktor suhu tinggi (HTS, dari bahan seperti REBCO) menghantar arus tanpa hambatan dan menghasilkan medan lebih kuat, sehingga reaktor dapat dibuat lebih kecil.

    Ukuran kemajuan fusi adalah Q, yaitu daya yang dihasilkan fusi dibagi daya yang dipakai untuk memanaskan plasma. Q di atas 1 berarti plasma menghasilkan lebih banyak energi daripada yang dimasukkan untuk memanaskannya. Namun ini belum berarti ada listrik bersih (net electricity), karena sistem juga memakai listrik untuk magnet, pendingin, dan alat bantu, dan hanya sebagian panas fusi yang berubah menjadi listrik. Fasilitas NIF di Amerika Serikat, yang menembakkan laser ke kapsul bahan bakar sebesar biji lada (cara ini disebut inersial), mencapai penguatan target di atas 1 pada 2022. ITER, reaktor tokamak eksperimen internasional di Prancis, mengikuti jadwal Baseline 2024: operasi riset dimulai 2034, operasi dengan deuterium saja 2035, dan operasi dengan deuterium–tritium penuh 2039. Setelah itu masih dibutuhkan pembangkit demonstrasi sebelum fusi bisa dipakai secara komersial.

    4.3 Fusi dan peta kekuasaan energi

    Bahan baku fusi, yaitu air laut dan litium, tersebar jauh lebih merata daripada minyak dan gas. Bila fusi komersial tercapai, logika geopolitik yang selama seabad berpusat pada sumur dan selat pelayaran akan melemah. Namun, rantai pasoknya akan berganti, bukan hilang: magnet HTS, tritium, litium, dan material yang tahan bombardir neutron akan menjadi kebutuhan baru. Dengan kata lain, ketergantungan berpindah ke tempat lain (Bab 5.2).

    4.4 Kepadatan energi, infrastruktur, dan logistik

    Nuklir berada di kelas kepadatan yang berbeda. Bahan bakar reaktor air ringan memakai uranium yang diperkaya: kadar uranium-235 dinaikkan dari 0,7% di alam menjadi sekitar 3–5%. Bahan bakar itu menyimpan sekitar 4 × 10⁶ MJ per kilogram dalam bentuk panas pada burnup tipikal (burnup adalah seberapa banyak energi diambil dari bahan bakar sebelum diganti). Akibatnya, satu pembangkit nuklir 1 GWe (gigawatt listrik) hanya membutuhkan sekitar 20–25 ton bahan bakar per tahun, kira-kira satu truk. Pembangkit batu bara sekelas membutuhkan sekitar 2–3 juta ton, yaitu puluhan kapal curah.

    Sebagai gantinya, infrastruktur nuklir terpusat dan berat: air pendingin dalam jumlah besar, zona pengaman di sekitar lokasi, siklus bahan bakar (pengayaan, pembuatan bahan bakar, penyimpanan bahan bakar bekas), dan jaringan transmisi berdaya gigawatt. Untuk fusi, kebutuhan bahan baku diperkirakan hanya ratusan kilogram deuterium dan tritium per tahun untuk pembangkit 1 GW. Namun penanganan tritium (radioaktif lemah, berumur paruh sekitar 12 tahun, dan mudah bocor karena molekulnya kecil) serta pasokan litium akan menjadi logistik baru.

    4.5 Konversi: bintang pun berakhir di turbin uap

    Di reaktor fisi yang paling umum, yaitu reaktor air bertekanan (PWR, pressurized water reactor), panas fisi memanaskan air di loop pertama sampai sekitar 300–325 °C. Tekanannya dijaga sekitar 155 bar supaya air tidak mendidih. Air panas ini mengalir ke steam generator, penukar panas yang menguapkan air di loop kedua pada tekanan sekitar 60–70 bar. Uap dari loop kedua itulah yang memutar turbin dan generator. Kedua loop sengaja dipisah agar air yang bersentuhan dengan teras reaktor tidak pernah menyentuh turbin.

    Karena suhu dan tekanan uapnya lebih rendah daripada boiler batu bara superkritis, efisiensi listriknya hanya sekitar 32–37% (ingat batas Carnot di 1.1). Reaktor generasi lanjut dengan pendingin helium atau garam leleh bersuhu 700–950 °C berpotensi memakai siklus yang lebih efisien (sekitar 40–50%), dan dapat memasok panas proses untuk hidrogen dan industri.

    Fusi punya jalur yang mirip. Pada reaksi D–T, sekitar 80% energi (14,1 dari 17,6 MeV) dibawa oleh neutron. Neutron menembus dinding plasma dan berhenti di lapisan berlitium yang disebut breeding blanket, tempat energinya berubah menjadi panas sekaligus membiakkan tritium. Panas itu dibawa pendingin ke penukar panas, uap, turbin, dan generator. Jadi “bintang di Bumi” pun bermuara pada rantai yang sama dengan mesin uap: Watt, Parsons, dan Faraday (2.1 dan 1.6). Yang berbeda hanya sumber panasnya. Satu pengecualian masih bersifat spekulatif, yaitu konsep mengambil listrik langsung dari partikel bermuatan pada bahan bakar tertentu.

    In plain words
    Nuclear energy comes from the nucleus of the atom, not from chemical bonds, so one kilogram holds millions of times more energy than ordinary fuel. There are two ways: fission (splitting heavy atoms such as uranium; used in today’s nuclear plants) and fusion (joining light atoms such as hydrogen; how the Sun works; still under development). One thing often surprises people: the end product is still heat that boils water and spins a steam turbine. This chapter explains how both work, their challenges, and how close fusion is to reality.

    4.1 Two ways to release nuclear energy: fission and fusion

    The atomic nucleus is held together by a very strong force. In a nuclear reaction, the mass of the products is slightly smaller than the mass at the start. That missing mass does not vanish; it becomes energy, according to Einstein’s famous formula:

    E = Δm · c²

    Read: energy equals the mass difference times the speed of light squared. Because the speed of light is enormous, even a tiny mass difference gives a huge amount of energy.

    Fission splits a heavy nucleus, such as uranium-235, into two smaller ones. As it splits, it also releases neutrons (particles with no charge), which can split other nuclei, creating a chain reaction. In a reactor, this chain reaction is kept under control by control rods that absorb neutrons. One kilogram of fully fissioned uranium-235 releases about 80 TJ (terajoules, 10¹² joules), roughly three million times the energy of a kilogram of coal. The price is spent fuel that is highly radioactive and must be guarded for thousands of years, plus strict reactor safety demands. One development path is the SMR (small modular reactor), a small reactor built in a factory and assembled on site, in the hope of cutting costs through repeated production.

    Fusion does the opposite: it joins two light nuclei into one heavier nucleus. The most promising fuels are deuterium (heavy hydrogen, available in seawater) and tritium (an even heavier hydrogen, rare, and bred from lithium). The D–T reaction produces helium, one neutron and 17.6 MeV of energy (MeV is a million electronvolts, an energy unit on the atomic scale). Fusion emits no CO₂ in operation, has no chain reaction that can run out of control (the reaction stops by itself if conditions are disturbed), and leaves far less long-lived waste than fission.

    4.2 Fusion: lighting a star in a bottle

    Two positively charged nuclei repel each other. To join them, they must be driven together at very high speed, which means extreme temperature. In the Sun, immense pressure helps, so about 15 million °C is enough. On Earth we cannot make such pressure, so the temperature must be far higher: about 100 million °C. At that temperature matter becomes plasma, a very hot gas in which electrons have been stripped from atoms so it carries electric charge. No wall can survive touching it, so the plasma must be held by magnetic fields, floating in the middle of the chamber.

    Three conditions must be met at once: the plasma must be hot enough, dense enough and confined long enough. Their product is called the triple product, and the minimum needed for fusion to produce energy is called the Lawson criterion. Two kinds of confinement device compete:

    • Tokamak: a doughnut-shaped chamber. Electric current inside the plasma helps shape the confining magnetic field.
    • Stellarator: a doughnut-shaped chamber whose field is made entirely by intricately twisted magnet coils. It is more stable but harder to build; an example is Wendelstein 7-X in Germany.

    High-temperature superconducting (HTS) magnets, made from materials such as REBCO, carry current without resistance and produce stronger fields, so reactors can be smaller.

    Fusion’s progress is measured by Q, the power produced by fusion divided by the power used to heat the plasma. Q above 1 means the plasma gives out more energy than was put in to heat it. But this does not yet mean net electricity, because the system also uses electricity for magnets, cooling and auxiliaries, and only part of the fusion heat becomes electricity. The NIF facility in the United States, which fires lasers at a fuel capsule the size of a peppercorn (an approach called inertial), reached a target gain above 1 in 2022. ITER, the international experimental tokamak in France, follows its 2024 Baseline schedule: research operations start in 2034, deuterium-only operation in 2035, and full deuterium–tritium operation in 2039. After that, demonstration power plants are still needed before fusion can be used commercially.

    4.3 Fusion and the map of energy power

    Fusion’s raw materials, seawater and lithium, are spread far more evenly than oil and gas. If commercial fusion arrives, the geopolitics that for a century revolved around wells and shipping straits would weaken. But the supply chain would change, not vanish: HTS magnets, tritium, lithium and materials that survive neutron bombardment would become new needs. In other words, dependence moves elsewhere (Chapter 5.2).

    4.4 Energy density, infrastructure and logistics

    Nuclear sits in a different density class. Light-water reactor fuel uses enriched uranium: the uranium-235 content is raised from 0.7% in nature to about 3–5%. That fuel stores about 4 × 10⁶ MJ per kilogram as heat at typical burnup (burnup is how much energy is taken from the fuel before it is replaced). As a result, a 1 GWe (gigawatt electric) nuclear plant needs only about 20–25 tonnes of fuel a year, roughly one truckload. A coal plant of the same size needs about 2–3 million tonnes, dozens of bulk ships.

    In return, nuclear infrastructure is centralised and heavy: large amounts of cooling water, exclusion zones around the site, a fuel cycle (enrichment, fuel fabrication, spent-fuel storage), and gigawatt-scale transmission. For fusion, fuel needs are estimated at only hundreds of kilograms of deuterium and tritium per year for a 1 GW plant. But handling tritium (weakly radioactive, with a half-life of about 12 years, and prone to leak because its molecules are small) and supplying lithium would be new logistics.

    RetailNo direct interaction; consumers only receive grid electricity
    Distribution pointsSubstations and high-voltage grids; district heating (hot-water pipes to buildings) where possible; SMRs or microreactors for remote sites
    Industrial scaleGW-class nuclear plants, enrichment and fuel fabrication facilities, spent-fuel storage, and tritium-breeding blankets in fusion

    4.5 Conversion: even a star ends in a steam turbine

    In the most common fission reactor, the pressurised water reactor (PWR), fission heat warms water in a first loop to about 300–325 °C. Its pressure is held at about 155 bar so the water does not boil. This hot water flows to a steam generator, a heat exchanger that boils water in a second loop at about 60–70 bar. Steam from the second loop spins the turbine and generator. The two loops are deliberately separate so that water touching the reactor core never reaches the turbine.

    Because steam temperature and pressure are lower than in a supercritical coal boiler, electrical efficiency is only about 32–37% (remember the Carnot limit in 1.1). Advanced reactors cooled by helium or molten salt at 700–950 °C could use more efficient cycles (about 40–50%) and supply process heat for hydrogen and industry.

    Fusion follows a similar path. In the D–T reaction, about 80% of the energy (14.1 of 17.6 MeV) is carried by neutrons. They pass through the plasma wall and stop in a lithium-bearing layer called the breeding blanket, where their energy becomes heat and tritium is bred at the same time. A coolant carries that heat to a heat exchanger, steam, turbine and generator. So the “star on Earth” ends up on the same chain as the steam engine: Watt, Parsons and Faraday (2.1 and 1.6). Only the heat source differs. One exception is still speculative: concepts that draw electricity directly from charged particles in certain fuels.

    Key points of Chapter IV
    Fission is commercial and extremely energy-dense, but needs heavy infrastructure and waste management. Fusion promises abundant fuel with no carbon emissions, but is still experimental: ITER’s schedule targets full deuterium–tritium operation in 2039. Both still end in a steam turbine. The first two axes (density and carbon) are met; what remains is the third: can it be produced at a scale and price that matter? That is the question of Chapter V.

Tidak ada kurva belajar berarti, sehingga harganya mengikuti harga bahan bakar, harga karbon, dan seberapa sering pembangkit dipakai (aset tetap dibagi kWh yang makin sedikit). Transisi (gas dengan CCS) turun pelan seiring pengalaman dan skala pusat CCS bersama. Hijau turun cepat lalu melandai, karena komponen non-modul (lahan, sambungan ke jaringan, biaya modal) dan batas fisik tidak ikut kurva belajar.

    Satu catatan penting: biaya per kWh yang murah belum berarti biaya sistem murah. Bila porsi VRE (terbarukan yang naik-turun) tinggi, integration cost (biaya tambahan agar jaringan sanggup menampungnya: jaringan, penyimpanan, cadangan) ikut naik. Itu sebabnya garis “surya + BESS” lebih relevan daripada surya saja bila yang dibandingkan adalah listrik yang andal (firm).

    5.7 Konversi, skala, dan sistem

    Dua keluarga konverter mengikuti ekonomi skala yang berbeda. Mesin panas (turbin, generator, boiler; lihat 2.1) sudah matang dan padat modal. Keuntungan skalanya ada pada ukuran unit: satu turbin besar lebih murah per kW daripada beberapa turbin kecil. Kurva belajarnya landai. Konverter padat dan elektrokimia (sel PV, baterai, elektroliser, inverter) bersifat modular dan diproduksi massal. Keuntungan skalanya ada pada jumlah unit, sehingga kurva belajarnya curam. Itu sebabnya harga kapasitas surya dan baterai turun jauh lebih cepat daripada harga turbin.

    Perbedaan ini punya akibat bagi seluruh sistem. Sekitar 90% listrik dunia pada 2025 masih lahir dari poros berputar (hitungan saya dari data Ember: 100% dikurangi surya PV 8,7%). Karena itu jaringan dirancang di sekitar kelembaman putar generator (inersia), arus hubung singkat, dan pengaturan frekuensi. Ketika porsi PV dan baterai naik, fungsi-fungsi itu harus disediakan dengan cara lain: grid-forming inverter, kondensor sinkron (mesin putar tanpa bahan bakar yang menambah inersia), dan cadangan. Biayanya masuk ke integration cost (5.6). Jadi, listrik memang carrier yang universal, tetapi sebagian besar elektron hari ini masih lahir dari tekanan uap, gas, atau air.

    In plain words
    The best technology in the lab does not necessarily win in the market. What decides it is the final price to the user. That price is shaped by three things: energy losses at each stage, the cost of building and running each stage (converting, delivering, storing), and how fast prices fall as production grows, which is called the learning curve. This chapter also covers important minerals such as lithium and nickel, compares the cost and efficiency of all sources in charts, then draws a picture of the world energy mix today and in 2050. The last part sums up the key messages of the whole book.

    5.1 The law of scale and the learning curve

    A technology’s price falls along two paths. The first is economies of scale: making one bigger unit or one bigger factory lowers the cost per unit. The second is the learning curve (also called Wright’s law): each time a product’s cumulative production doubles, its cost falls by a roughly fixed percentage. That percentage is called the learning rate (LR). For example at an LR of 20%, cost goes from 100 to 80 when production doubles, then to 64, 51 and so on.

    Relative cost (start = 100) by number of doublings of cumulative production. Illustrative calculation, not data for any one product.

    Solar panels (photovoltaic, PV) have an LR of about 20–24% and lithium-ion batteries about 18–20%. The result is real: according to BNEF, the average price of a battery pack fell about 93% since 2010 (from ± USD 1,474 per kWh in 2025 dollars) to USD 108 per kWh in 2025. For stationary storage the price is USD 70/kWh, LFP packs USD 81/kWh and NMC USD 128/kWh.

    Technologies still early on the curve, such as green hydrogen, SMRs and fusion, face the opposite problem. A first-of-a-kind (FOAK) project costs far more than an nth-of-a-kind (NOAK) one. Many technologies get stuck in the “valley of death”, the period after demonstration and before mass production, when costs are still high and funders hesitate. This is where the cost of capital (the return funders demand) matters: technologies that are expensive up front and cheap later, such as solar, wind and nuclear, are very sensitive to it.

So the geopolitical issue shifts from the maritime chokepoints of oil (narrow shipping passages, for example straits) to control of refining and manufacturing. For countries producing raw materials, the question is the value chain: do they only sell raw material, or also process it into intermediates, battery cells and recycling? Each level needs refining electricity (carbon footprint) and has different costs. Recycling is an important complement, since metal already mined can be used again.

    5.3 Logistics as a determinant of scale

    Delivered cost is the cost of energy that actually reaches the user: production cost plus transport, storage and final conversion. Think of the price of vegetables: cheap at the farm, dearer in the city. A cheap source in a distant place is not necessarily cheap where it is used. Infrastructure assets such as pipelines, cables and terminals also last decades, so they risk becoming stranded assets (assets left unused before their capital is repaid) if demand shifts.

    The three delivery scales have different economic logic. Retail values convenience and reliability. Distribution points value using assets as fully as possible. Industry values cost per unit at large volume. For island or remote regions, the last mile (the final stretch to the user) is a particular problem: an island dependent on diesel generators and fuel shipments may be better served by a solar microgrid with batteries, as long as spare parts and maintenance are assured.

    5.4 The economic burden of the energy chain

    Efficiency is only half the story. Every stage of the energy chain loses some energy, and that lost energy has already been paid for. Every stage also adds capital cost, operating cost and financing cost. Simply put:

    Cservice ≈ Cinput ÷ ηtotal + Cconversion + Cdistribution + Cstorage

    Read: the cost of the final service equals the cost of the energy put in (divided by total efficiency, so it swells when much is lost), plus the cost of each stage. The three stages are:

    • Conversion: converters (turbines, electrolysers, fuel cells) carry a capital cost per kW and depend on how often they run. An electrolyser that only runs when solar is plentiful has a low capacity factor, so capital cost per kg of H₂ balloons.
    • Distribution: cables, pipes and substations have high fixed costs paid through tariffs. In many power systems, transmission and distribution make up about a third or more of the final bill.
    • Storage: its cost has two parts, per kW (power) and per kWh (energy). The average measure is called LCOS (levelized cost of storage) and depends on the number of cycles, lifetime and duration.

    Three drivers shape these paths. The learning curve (5.1) works by the formula C = C₀ · (X ÷ X₀)−b, with learning rate LR = 1 − 2−b. At an LR of 20%, if cumulative capacity doubles every 3 years, component cost by calculation falls about 80% in 25 years, but only about 60% if it doubles every 6 years. That gap is the value of adoption and scale. The third driver is the cost of capital and carbon policy, which can shift the whole curve.

    Fossil is mature. There is no significant learning curve, so its price follows fuel prices, carbon prices and how often plants are used (fixed assets divided by fewer kWh). Transition (gas with CCS) falls slowly with experience and the scale of shared CCS hubs. Green falls fast then flattens, because non-module components (land, grid connection, cost of capital) and physical limits do not follow the learning curve.

    One important note: a low cost per kWh does not mean a low system cost. When the share of VRE (variable renewables) is high, integration cost (the extra cost for the grid to absorb them: grids, storage, backup) rises too. That is why the “solar + BESS” line is more relevant than solar alone when comparing reliable (firm) electricity.

    5.7 Conversion, scale and the system

    Two families of converters follow different economies of scale. Heat engines (turbines, generators, boilers; see 2.1) are mature and capital-heavy. Their scale advantage lies in unit size: one big turbine is cheaper per kW than several small ones. Their learning curve is shallow. Solid-state and electrochemical converters (PV cells, batteries, electrolysers, inverters) are modular and mass-produced. Their scale advantage lies in number of units, so their learning curve is steep. That is why the price of solar and battery capacity fell far faster than the price of turbines.

    This difference has consequences for the whole system. About 90% of the world’s electricity in 2025 still came from spinning shafts (my calculation from Ember data: 100% minus solar PV’s 8.7%). So grids are designed around the rotating inertia of generators, short-circuit current and frequency regulation. As the share of PV and batteries rises, those functions must be provided another way: grid-forming inverters, synchronous condensers (spinning machines without fuel that add inertia) and backup. The cost goes into integration cost (5.6). So electricity is indeed a universal carrier, but most electrons today are still born from the pressure of steam, gas or water.

    5.8 The world energy mix: today and 2050

    There are two ways to look at “who supplies the world’s energy”. First, all the energy people use, from gasoline to factory heat and electricity; this is called primary energy and is measured in EJ (exajoules, 10¹⁸ joules, or one billion GJ). Second, electricity alone, measured in TWh (billion kWh). In 2025 the world used about 600 EJ of primary energy. To picture it, divide that by the number of seconds in a year to get average power: about 19 TW (trillion watts), equal to 19 billion 1,000-watt heaters running all year. The formula: average power = annual energy ÷ seconds in a year; 1 EJ per year is about 31.7 GW.

    How to read the 2050 numbers

    The 2050 numbers are scenarios: “what if” cases with certain assumptions, not predictions. The IEA uses several paths: CPS (Current Policies Scenario, only policies already in force), STEPS (Stated Policies Scenario, policies governments have announced) and NZE (Net Zero Emissions by 2050, a path to net-zero emissions). IRENA and DNV have their own paths. Patterns that appear in almost all of them:

    1. Electricity grows. In STEPS the world needs about 40% more electricity by 2035; electricity rises from 21% to about 31% of final energy by 2050; the renewable share of electricity rises from about 32% (2024) to nearly 70%.
    2. Solar grows fastest. Its capacity in STEPS rises from ± 2,160 GW (2024) to ± 16,200 GW (2050).
    3. The fossil share falls, the volume not necessarily. Total energy rises too. STEPS sees coal and oil peaking around 2030, CPS sees oil and gas still growing to 2050, and industry outlooks such as ExxonMobil’s expect oil and gas to still supply more than half of world energy in 2050.

    Pola lintas penggunaan

    Pertama, pembakaran tetap kuat di dua wilayah: suhu sangat tinggi dan gerak berbobot dengan jarak jauh. Di luar itu, tugasnya sebagian besar dapat dilakukan lebih efisien oleh listrik. Kedua, elektrifikasi memangkas kebutuhan energi primer karena motor listrik dan pompa panas tidak dibatasi Carnot seperti mesin kalor: efisiensi peralatan naik dua hingga empat kali, dan penghematan itu sering lebih besar daripada tambahan pasokan hijau yang dibutuhkan. Ketiga, skala menentukan jalur: pada skala retail (induksi, kendaraan listrik, pompa panas) hambatan utamanya kapasitas jaringan distribusi; pada skala industri (baja, semen, kaca, amonia) hambatannya suhu, bahan baku, dan keekonomian, sehingga hidrogen dan CCS difokuskan di sana. Dengan demikian pembakaran hidrogen (Bab 3.7) berfungsi paling logis di sisa ceruk yang sulit dielektrifikasi, bukan sebagai pengganti gas di setiap dapur.

    In plain words
    People do not buy energy; they buy what it does: a cool room, cooked food, a journey, light, pumped water and factory goods. Each of these needs can be met by combustion or by electricity. This section shows that for many needs the electric route uses two to four times less energy, but combustion remains ahead for very high temperatures and heavy long-distance vehicles.

    Users do not buy joules; they buy services: a cool room, cooked food, travel, light, pumped water, manufactured goods. Each service demands a particular useful form of energy (heat at a certain temperature, mechanical work, or light), and this is where the combustion route and the electric route compete.

    Rounded values at equipment level. The electric route does not yet count generation and transmission losses; heating uses a COP of ± 3 (a heat pump moves heat, so it exceeds 100%).

    Patterns across uses

    First, combustion stays strong in two areas: very high temperatures and heavy movement over long distances. Beyond those, electricity can do most jobs more efficiently. Second, electrification cuts primary energy needs because electric motors and heat pumps are not bound by Carnot like heat engines: equipment efficiency rises two to four times, and those savings are often larger than the extra green supply needed. Third, scale decides the route: at retail scale (induction, electric vehicles, heat pumps) the main obstacle is distribution-grid capacity; at industrial scale (steel, cement, glass, ammonia) the obstacles are temperature, feedstock and economics, so hydrogen and CCS are focused there. Hydrogen combustion (Chapter 3.7) therefore works most logically in the leftover niches that are hard to electrify, not as a replacement for gas in every kitchen.

    Arah yang lebih baik. Empat prinsip mengikat seluruh buku. Pertama, cocokkan carrier dengan tugasnya: elektron untuk yang ringan dan harian, molekul untuk yang berat, jauh, dan panas (Bab III). Kedua, kurangi kerugian sebelum menambah pasokan: motor listrik dan pompa panas melipatgandakan efisiensi layanan (Penggunaan Akhir). Ketiga, jaringan, penyimpanan, dan mineral adalah prasyarat, bukan pelengkap (Bab V). Keempat, ukur kemajuan dengan tiga sumbu sekaligus: densitas, karbon, dan delivered cost (biaya energi sampai ke pengguna, termasuk pengiriman), ditambah keandalan dan akses.

    Untuk setiap negara. Titik awalnya berbeda-beda: negara yang kaya bahan tambang, sungai, atau panas bumi, yang berbentuk kepulauan, atau yang bergantung pada impor bahan bakar akan memilih bauran yang berbeda. Pembangkit skala kecil, mikrogrid, dan pengolahan bahan mentah menjadi bagian dari jawaban di banyak tempat. Dua tantangan umum adalah jejak karbon listrik untuk pengolahan mineral dan perawatan jangka panjang pembangkit lokal.

    Dari api menuju bintang bukan lintasan lurus, melainkan jembatan bertingkat: setiap tahap membeli waktu bagi tahap berikutnya.

    Where we stand. In 2025, according to Ember, renewables overtook coal in the global power mix for the first time, and solar alone met about three quarters of demand growth. Balance matters: Lazard’s 2026 analysis shows costs rising across almost every technology (capital, interest rates, tariffs), new gas build-out is growing on data-centre and electrification demand, and US stand-alone storage costs rose with cell tariffs. Solar and wind remain the cheapest new capacity, yet firm capacity is still needed. The honest conclusion for this decade: no single winner, but a shifting mix.

    A better direction. Four principles bind the book. First, match the carrier to the task: electrons for the light and daily, molecules for the heavy, distant and hot. Second, cut losses before adding supply: electric motors and heat pumps multiply the efficiency of services. Third, grids, storage and minerals are prerequisites, not extras. Fourth, measure progress on three axes at once: density, carbon and delivered cost, plus reliability and access.

    For every country. Starting points differ: countries rich in minerals, rivers or geothermal heat, island nations, and fuel importers will choose different mixes. Small plants, microgrids and local processing of raw materials are part of the answer in many places. Two common challenges are the carbon footprint of the power used for mineral processing and long-term maintenance of local plants.

    From fire to the stars is not a straight line but a staircase of bridges: each stage buys time for the next.
